HPLC vs mass spectrometry: what each one tells you

A side-by-side look at two analytical methods that appear on peptide paperwork, and the different question each one answers.

Two different questions

HPLC asks how many separable components a sample contains and how much of the signal belongs to the main one. Mass spectrometry asks what the mass of each component is. Because the questions differ, the two methods complement each other rather than compete.

What HPLC measures

In high-performance liquid chromatography, a dissolved sample is pushed through a column and its components leave at different times. A detector, commonly UV absorbance, records a trace of peaks. The area of the main peak relative to all peaks is often reported as purity. That figure depends on the column, solvents, gradient and detection wavelength, so it is only meaningful alongside the method.

What mass spectrometry measures

A mass spectrometer measures the mass-to-charge ratio of ions. For a peptide, the measured mass can be compared with the mass calculated from its sequence to help confirm identity. Peptides often appear with several charges, so the instrument reports a series of signals that are converted back to a single molecular mass.

What neither one shows alone

An HPLC trace does not say what its main peak is, and a mass spectrum on its own does not say how much of the sample the main component makes up. Some impurities can sit under the main peak or be invisible to the detector used, and the two methods are often run together, sometimes coupled as LC-MS.

Reading paperwork with both

When a document lists both, check that the stated method, lot number, date and testing laboratory are present, and that the lot matches the one on your vial. A purity percentage with no method, or a mass with no expected value to compare it to, is incomplete.
